# Predicting Air Quality in India: next-day AQI forecasting for 453 stations

> **How this report is maintained.** Every results table sits between `<!-- TABLE:name -->` markers and is regenerated by `python src/report_tables.py`, the last pipeline step, from the files the run produced. The interpretation paragraphs were written from the full run of 27 Nov 2026 (commit `ce48258`, see `run_manifest.json`). After any re-run the tables update automatically, but these paragraphs must be checked against them.

## 1. Problem and task definition

| | |
|---|---|
| **Unit of prediction** | station × day |
| **Target (regression)** | AQI of day D+1 at that station, **computed by this project from the CPCB National AQI breakpoints** (section 3.3). It is not an officially published station AQI. |
| **Target (classification)** | **"Poor or worse"**: AQI(D+1) > 200, the CPCB band at which health advisories start |
| **Forecast issue time** | 24:00 IST of day D. Every input must be available by then (section 5). |
| **Horizon** | 1 day ahead (D → D+1) |
| **Two ways to classify** | (a) *derived* from the AQI forecast, both at the fixed rule "> 200" and at an AQI threshold tuned on 2021; (b) *direct*: a class-weighted classifier whose score threshold is tuned on 2021 |

**Why this target.** The AQI is what the public and regulators act on, and one day ahead leaves time to issue an advisory or a GRAP restriction. "Poor or worse" is a minority class, so imbalance-aware metrics are used throughout (section 6).

**Scope of the claims.** This is a **retrospective forecasting study** on archived data. It is not a deployed system: the app replays history, and some auxiliary data (FIRMS fire locations) are the archived standard product rather than what was available in real time (section 2.3).

## 2. Data

### 2.1 Sources

The full provenance, licences and caveats are in [data_sources.md](data_sources.md).

* **CPCB CCR hourly monitoring** (primary): 453 stations in **242 cities**, counted as distinct (state, city) pairs, because "Aurangabad" exists in both Bihar and Maharashtra (241 distinct city names). It runs from Jan 2010 to Mar 2023, about 14.3 M hourly rows, with 11 pollutants and 7 on-site weather channels. It comes from the Kaggle compilation `abhisheksjha/time-series-air-quality-data-of-india-2010-2023` (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).
* **Station coordinates:**
  * **OpenAQ** station coordinates, where a CPCB station matches an OpenAQ location by name within 60 km of its city;
  * otherwise the city centre (GeoNames via the Open-Meteo geocoder, plus 14 manually verified towns), flagged in `stations_geo.csv` as `coord_source`.
* **NASA FIRMS MODIS C6.1** active fires over India, 2010–2023.
* **Diwali dates**, 2010–2023.

**Prediction vs evaluation.** A forecast can be made for any grid day with history, but it can only be *evaluated* where AQI(D+1) was measured. All scores are therefore conditional on the station reporting the next day.

### 2.3 Alignment in time and space

* **Time:** everything is aggregated to **IST calendar days**.
  * FIRMS acquisition times are UTC. They are converted to IST, and a **3-hour near-real-time latency** is added: a detection counts for the first issue day on which it would already have been available. A 22:30 IST night overpass therefore counts for the *next* day.
* **Space:** fire counts are great-circle counts within 100 km and 300 km of the station coordinates, plus a Punjab + Haryana regional total (the stubble-burning signal).
  * Stations without verified station coordinates use their city centre, so stations in the same city share fire features.
* **Operational realism:**
  * The FIRMS archive is the standard-quality product, which replaces the near-real-time records later.
  * We use only fields the near-real-time product also has: location, time, confidence, FRP. The standard-only `type` field is not used.

## 3. Cleaning, sensor-fault detection and EDA

### 3.1 Timestamps and units (`src/clean.py`)

* **Timestamps.** All 453 files were checked: exactly hourly, on the hour, with no duplicates. The code still averages any sub-hourly, off-the-hour or duplicated readings into their clock hour and logs the count (`cadence_log.csv`), so such data could never be silently dropped.
* **Units.**
  * Every observed (variable, unit) pair is inventoried in `unit_handling.csv`, and conversions use 25 °C and 1 atm.
  * Pollutant columns with a unit that has **no verified conversion are rejected and logged**, not assumed.
  * Blank units are assumed to be the canonical unit, and logged.
  * Met-channel headers are often mislabelled on the CPCB portal, so their unit text is ignored and the physical-range checks apply.
  * In this dataset no rejected units occur. The risky combinations (NO2/SO2/Toluene in ppb, CO/NH3 in ppm) are absent.
* **Pressure.** Units are decided **per reading**: values above 850 are hPa, mmHg values lie below 800. Nothing depends on other days.

### 3.2 Sensor-fault QA

Rules (thresholds in `src/config.py`) look only at one reading or at the same calendar day:

| Rule | Definition |
|---|---|
| Out of physical range | outside `VALID_RANGE`, e.g. PM2.5 outside 0.01–1,500 µg/m³ |
| PM2.5 > 1.5 × PM10 | the PM2.5 reading is removed (humidity artefacts of PM2.5 monitors are the usual cause; the rule cannot prove which sensor is wrong) |
| Stuck sensor | ≥ 8 identical consecutive hours, **only above a low level** (`STUCK_MIN_VALUE`); flat runs near the detection limit are kept |
| Isolated spike | > 4 × the day median and above a floor, **with both neighbouring hours observed** and below half the value |

**Do the rules delete genuine episodes?** `AQI_noqa` recomputes the AQI without the stuck, spike and PM-ratio rules, and per-day removal counts are kept. The table compares severe and Poor-or-worse days, and Diwali days, with and without QA:

* **Overall:** 0.73% of station-days change category, and 2,171 station-days (0.5%) lose a valid AQI.

### 3.3 Daily aggregation and the AQI

* **Daily values:**
  * a daily mean needs ≥ 16 valid hours;
  * CO and O3 use the maximum 8-hour running mean, where each window needs ≥ 6 valid hours and the day ≥ 16 (a daily *maximum* over a few hours would be biased low);
  * evening (18–23 h) means need ≥ 4 of the 6 hours.
* **AQI** (`src/aqi.py`): the CPCB band formula, I = I_lo + (I_hi − I_lo)/(C_hi − C_lo) · (C − C_lo), applied within the band of C.
  * Concentrations are first rounded to integers (CO to 0.1 mg/m³), so the published integer bands are contiguous. For example, PM2.5 = 31 → 51 and PM2.5 = 91 → 201; this is tested.
  * The Severe band is open-ended in the CPCB table. Its upper concentration (e.g. 380 µg/m³ for PM2.5) is a **project convention**, and values above it are capped at 500.
  * The AQI requires ≥ 3 sub-indices, including PM2.5 or PM10.

### 3.4 Key EDA findings

Figures are `figures/eda_01` to `eda_12`, and numbers are in `eda_summary.json`. Precise definitions:
* **Spatial dependence** is the median Pearson correlation of **daily PM2.5** between station pairs, by distance band (2019 onwards).
* **The Diwali effect** is the **North-region mean PM2.5** relative to its mean over days −10 to −4, with the median taken over years.
* **Class balance** is the share of station-days with AQI > 200, overall and by region.

The calendar grid has 595,889 station-days, of which 445,079 have a valid AQI.

* **Class balance:** 24.0% of valid station-days are Poor or worse (Poor 9.9%, Very Poor 11.1%, Severe 3.0%).
  * The share varies strongly by region: North (IGP) 38.7%, East 19.7%, North-East & Hills 14.1%, West 13.1%, Central 12.0% and South 2.1%.
  * The AQI is right-skewed: median 111, 90th percentile 320, 99th percentile 489.
* **What drives the AQI:** PM10 is the dominant sub-index on 42.6% of days and PM2.5 on 35.4%. On Poor-or-worse days, however, PM2.5 dominates 80.4% of the time, so the alert task is essentially a PM2.5 task.
* **Seasonality** (`eda_05`): every region peaks in winter and is lowest in the monsoon. In the North, mean PM2.5 rises from about 34 µg/m³ in August to about 169 µg/m³ in November; the South stays between about 19 and 47 µg/m³.
* **Missingness is not random** (`eda_04`):
  * A station's PM2.5 is missing on 28.3–29.4% of days when the other stations in its city are Poor, Very Poor or Severe, against 22.0–24.4% on Good to Moderate days.
  * Gaps are therefore more frequent on polluted days. This is why raw missingness flags are features, and it means that scores conditional on a reported target slightly under-represent polluted days.
  * Overall missingness ranges from 12.7% (NO2) to 44.1% (Toluene); PM2.5 is missing on 18.9% of days and PM10 on 26.1%.
* **Spatial dependence** (`eda_09`): the median correlation of daily PM2.5 between station pairs falls with distance.
  * It is 0.87 below 50 km, 0.76 at 50–100 km, 0.66 at 100–250 km, 0.49 at 500–1,000 km and still 0.40 beyond 1,000 km.
  * Stations on the same day are far from independent. This is why validation is chronological, the intervals use weekly blocks and the spatial hold-outs use a 100 km buffer.
* **Diwali** (`eda_11`): in the North, mean PM2.5 on the day after Diwali is 1.53 times its level 4–10 days before (median over years).

## 4. Missing data and imputation

### 4.1 What the pipeline does

1. **The target is never imputed.**
2. **History used for lag features** (`features.causal_fill`) is filled on the **complete daily calendar grid** of each station, using only information up to day D:
   1. the mean of the other stations in the same (state, city) that day;
   2. otherwise, forward-fill of at most **3 calendar days**.
3. **Remaining gaps** are filled by `prep.Imputer`, which is **fitted inside every fold** on that fold's training rows only. The fallback order is station × month median → city × month → region × month → global median.
4. **Raw missingness flags** are taken *before* any fill: valid-hour counts, `miss_aqi_l0`, `days_since_valid`, and `isna_PM25_mean_l0`, `isna_PM10_mean_l0`, `isna_aqi_l1`, `isna_city_aqi_l0`, `isna_site_WS_l0`.

Because all inputs are row-wise functions of data up to D, a station-day gets **identical features whether or not its next-day target exists**. The training rows and the app's rows are verified to be equal (section 5.1).

### 4.2 Comparing strategies (`src/imputation.py`)

* **Reconstruction.**
  * Known daily AQI and PM2.5 values are hidden **in 2021 only**, as disjoint, calendar-contiguous gaps of 1–7 days on fully observed stretches, covering about 10% of the observed values (the achieved fraction is reported). Reference statistics come from data ≤ 2020. There are 3 random seeds.
  * The **deployed** function is the one evaluated.
  * Errors are reported on each method's own coverage *and* on the rows every method covers.

**Choice:**
* **History:** the hybrid fill (same-city neighbours, then forward-fill of at most 3 days, then the fold-fitted climatology).
* **Remaining gaps:** the hierarchical climatology.

**Why.**
* **Reconstruction (2021):** the hybrid is the best *causal* method that covers every gap.
  * It reaches AQI MAE 32.9 (PM2.5: 16.8 µg/m³) at 100% coverage, against 47.9 (25.5) for station-month climatology.
  * Forward-fill and neighbours alone cover only 65% and 58% of the hidden values.
  * On the rows that every method covers, the hybrid (31.0) is as accurate as linear interpolation (31.4), which uses the future and is not allowed in forecasting.
* **By gap length:** the hybrid's error grows from 29.0 for 1-day gaps to about 34 for 6–7-day gaps. Forward-fill alone is best for 1-day gaps (27.2) but worse from 2 days on.
* **Downstream, the choice matters very little.**
  * The four history fills give 2021 validation MAE 24.31–24.40 and F1 0.844–0.845; the three residual strategies give 24.25–24.35.
  * These spreads (at most 0.1 AQI, under 0.4%) come from single fits without intervals, so they are treated as ties.
  * A plausible reason, not tested: the models also see the missingness flags, `days_since_valid` and the city features, so they can compensate for imperfect fills.
* **Decision:** with the downstream results tied, it rests on reconstruction and on the hybrid being causal with full coverage. The residual strategy stays at the planned hierarchical climatology. The global median scored 0.09 lower in this single XGBoost fit, which is within the tie margin, and it was not tested with the learners that cannot handle missing values natively.

**Weather statement** (required by the brief): we use **observed weather up to the issue time only**, from the station's own live sensors. Weather of the target day is never used, and there is no weather-forecast input. ERA5 reanalysis was rejected because it is published about 5 days late.

The offline test suite (`tests/`) checks the same properties on synthetic data, as well as the AQI band boundaries and each QA rule.

### 5.2 Splits and protocol

* **Train** ≤ 2020, **validation** 2021, **test** Jan 2022 – Mar 2023.
* **Screening:** walk-forward, using two folds:

  | Fold | Train | Validate |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1 | ≤ 2019 | 2020 |
  | 2 | ≤ 2020 | 2021 |

  * Within each fold, the imputer and climatology, the class weights and the PCA are **all refitted on that fold's training rows**.
  * Grids are screened on a 40% sample of each fold's training rows, stratified by station × year, for compute reasons. Grids include the loss/objective for the regressors.
  * Boosters (AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost) choose their number of rounds on the chronological validation year from staged predictions. There is no random internal holdout.
  * PCA models are tuned **separately** with the same budget.
* **Finalist:** the chosen setting is refitted on **all** rows ≤ 2020 and validated on 2021. This fixes the number of rounds, the classification thresholds (F1-maximising, from an exact precision–recall search) and the validation score.
* **Production model:** the best **2021 validation** score. The test set is not used to choose anything.
* **Final:** refit on ≤ 2021, then evaluated on the test period.
* **Planned test analyses:** benchmark, backtest, error analysis, spatial hold-outs and the severe-day variants are all *pre-specified* evaluations on the test period. Model, feature, threshold and imputation choices use validation data only. The ablation uses the validation year.

### 5.3 Spatial dependence (`src/spatial_cv.py`)

* **Scenario:** a new city or region whose monitors already report live data, but none of whose history was available for training.
* **Hold-out design:**
  * Held-out stations are removed from model fitting and from the imputer and climatology.
  * They are also excluded as **donors** of the neighbour features of training rows.
  * Training additionally drops every station within **100 km** (buffer).
* **Two designs:** buffered leave-one-city-out (12 large cities) and leave-one-region-out (6 regions).
* **Comparison:** the "seen" comparison model uses **identical settings**, evaluated on identical rows, with 95% block-bootstrap intervals.

### 5.4 Baselines

* **Persistence:** today's *measured* AQI. If it is missing, the last measured AQI is used if at most 7 days old; otherwise the seasonal climatology. The result is always finite.
* **Seasonal climatology:** mean AQI of the target month (station → city → region → all), fitted on the **same cutoff** as the models.

## 6. Models and benchmark

Models: Decision Tree, Random Forest (150 trees), AdaBoost (decision-tree base learners), Gradient Boosting (sklearn) and XGBoost (`hist`). Each runs on raw features and on **StandardScaler + PCA (95% variance, fitted inside every fit)**.

Imbalance handling for the direct classifiers, computed on the rows being fitted:
* `class_weight="balanced"` for the Decision Tree;
* `"balanced_subsample"` for the Random Forest;
* balanced sample weights for AdaBoost and Gradient Boosting;
* `scale_pos_weight` for XGBoost.

Metrics:
* **regression:** MAE, RMSE, R², error on the top 5% of days, and MAE / bias / recall on Severe days (AQI > 400);
* **classification:** precision, recall, F1, **AP** (`average_precision_score`, the step-wise area under the precision–recall curve), ROC-AUC, false-alert rate and Brier score.

**PCA hurts every model on every task.**
* **Dimension:** keeping 95% of the variance needs 44 components for 83 features; the first component alone explains 34%.
* **Regression:** test MAE rises by 2.36 (XGBoost) to 3.54 (Decision Tree), and every paired interval excludes 0 (table above).

**A plausible mechanism, not a proven one.**
* Components ordered by variance need not align with the few columns that carry the predictive signal (AQI and pollutant history).
* Rotation also removes the convenient original-axis split structure that trees use.
* Both boosters needed many more rounds with PCA (XGBoost 1,495 vs 305) and reached or nearly reached the cap. This is consistent with a harder-to-split representation, but does not prove it.

  * AQI plus pollutant history comes within 0.4 of the full model for the boosters (XGBoost 24.05 vs 23.64), and matches it for the Decision Tree (25.75 vs 25.76).

**Fitting costs differ by two orders of magnitude; prediction costs hardly matter.**

* **Fitting** (final regressors, trained on data up to 2021):
  * XGBoost is both the most accurate and among the cheapest: 20 s wall time (209 CPU-seconds on 12 threads).
  * The Decision Tree takes 15 s and AdaBoost 212 s (10 rounds).
  * Random Forest takes 269 s wall time but 2,449 CPU-seconds, the most work.
  * Single-threaded Gradient Boosting takes 2,121 s for 354 rounds. It costs about 10× XGBoost's CPU time (and about 100× its wall time) for an MAE 0.16 worse.
  * Among the classifiers, AdaBoost is the slowest (1,613 s) and XGBoost the fastest (10 s).
* **Model size:**
  * Random Forest models are 107 MB (regression) and 28 MB (classification); every other model is under 2.6 MB.
  * This matters at cold start: loading Random Forest and predicting one row takes 1.5 s, against 11–113 ms for the others.
* **Latency:**
  * One row on one thread takes 0.06 ms (Decision Tree) to 7.6 ms (Random Forest); a whole day of 395 stations takes 0.08–27 ms.
  * End to end, with the imputer, a day's batch takes 41–106 ms for every model, so the imputer dominates.
  * Every model can forecast all stations in well under a second, so inference cost is not a reason to prefer one model.
* **Learner-sample curve:**
  * With only 10% of the rows up to 2020, XGBoost already reaches 24.12 on 2021. That beats the Decision Tree, AdaBoost and Random Forest trained on 100% (25.76, 25.05 and 24.32).
  * Going from 10% to 100% of the rows improves MAE by 0.26 (AdaBoost) to 0.98 (Decision Tree). The last doubling (50% → 100%) gains at most 0.17, and nothing for AdaBoost (25.03 vs 25.05). Seed-to-seed ranges reach 0.22.
  * Fit time grows roughly in proportion to the rows for Gradient Boosting (×11.6 for ×10 rows) and faster for Random Forest (×16), but only ×3.7 for XGBoost.
  * Two hypotheses, neither tested: the flattening curve suggests that more rows of the same kind add little, and XGBoost's sub-linear time may come from a fixed per-round overhead.
* **Trade-off:**
  * XGBoost dominates: the best accuracy, a fast fit, a small model and fast prediction.
  * Gradient Boosting is nearly as accurate (+0.16 MAE) but about 100× slower to fit on one thread.
  * Random Forest needs many cores and much memory, and the Decision Tree is the cheapest but least accurate.

**Where the models fail** (figures `err_01` to `err_09`):

* **Pollution level:** error grows with how polluted a place and season are.
  * XGBoost's MAE is 13.0 in the South and 30.3 in the North (IGP).
  * Across 403 test stations, station MAE correlates 0.88 with the station's mean AQI.
  * By month, January is the worst (32.0) and July the best (12.4). Weekdays differ little (23.7–24.8).
  * The gain over persistence is largest where pollution is highest: 17% in the IGP, against 10% in the South and 7% in the North-East & Hills.
* **Pull toward the middle.** The bias is forecast minus truth.
  * By *true* category, XGBoost's bias is positive on Good to Moderate days (+4 to +9) and negative on Poor-or-worse days (−17 on Poor, −23 on Very Poor, −64 on Severe).
  * By *forecast* category, the bias is small up to Poor (−3.3 to +1.5) but positive when the model forecasts Very Poor (+6.3) or Severe (+10.3).
  * So high truths are under-forecast, while high forecasts are on average slightly too high: the usual regression-to-the-mean pattern, which is why both views are reported.
* **Change from day to day:**
  * When the AQI barely changes (by less than 25), persistence is better (9.3 vs 12.3 MAE).
  * For large changes the models are far better (above 150: 126 vs 191), but the errors stay very large.
  * The models forecast the *decay* of an episode well: when today is Severe, XGBoost's MAE is 48 against persistence's 64.
  * They forecast its *onset* poorly: the derived rule catches 0.32–0.35 of onsets and the direct classifiers up to 0.41.
* **Alerts by slice** (XGBoost direct classifier):
  * Recall is 0.89 in winter but 0.63 in the monsoon, and only 0.30 in the South, where Poor-or-worse days are rare (493 of 28,064).
  * Recall is 0.96–0.98 on Very Poor and Severe days but 0.72 on Poor days: most misses are days just above 200.
* **Special days:**
  * Diwali ±2 days have MAE 37.6, against 24.2 on other days.
  * When AQI(D) is missing, the models lose much less than persistence (28.6 vs 39.8).
* **Stations:**
  * Stations with no training rows have a *lower* XGBoost MAE (21.8 vs 24.7), but persistence is lower too (24.1 vs 29.1): these station-days are simply less polluted. Their gain over persistence shrinks from 15% to 10%, and classifier recall falls from 0.85 to 0.77.
  * XGBoost is worse than persistence at 24 of 403 stations. Most are very clean, stable stations where the AQI hardly moves (e.g. Vijayapura: 2.1 vs 0.7). The other notable case is Byrnihat (mean AQI 418: 72 vs 56).
  * City-averaged MAE is lower than pooled MAE (22.9 vs 24.4), because pooling gives weight to the many polluted Delhi stations. The ranking is unchanged.
* **The models fail together:** the errors of the learned models correlate 0.93–0.98 with one another, but only 0.80–0.83 with persistence, so averaging the learned models would add little.
* **Calibration** (`err_09`):
  * The classifier scores overstate the probability. For Gradient Boosting and XGBoost, days scored around 0.55 are Poor or worse only about 30% of the time.
  * This is the expected effect of balanced class weights. AdaBoost's curve is S-shaped, and Brier scores are 0.062–0.087.
  * The app therefore shows them as scores, and decisions use thresholds tuned on validation (0.59–0.70).
* **Prediction intervals:**
  * The 80% intervals cover 81.0% of test days overall and 78.5–82.5% by season.
  * They cover only 70% of Poor days and 53% of Severe days. The intervals are built per *forecast* category, and the forecasts are too low on extreme days.

**Severe-day variants.** On 2021 validation, every variant that targets extremes helps Severe days but costs ordinary days.
* **Quantile 0.7:**
  * Cuts Severe-day MAE from 62.9 to 51.7 and raises Severe recall from 0.56 to 0.66.
  * Raises overall MAE by 3.1 and the Poor false-alert rate from 4.1% to 6.8%.
* **Weight 5:** Severe-day MAE 56.3, overall MAE +1.7, false alerts 6.5%.
* **Delta** (learning the change from today's AQI):
  * The only variant that passed the rule fixed in advance: validation MAE 23.61 vs 23.64, Severe MAE 59.4 vs 62.9, Severe recall 0.58 vs 0.56. It therefore replaces the production regressor.
  * On the test period, the Severe-day gain carried over but is small: Severe MAE 67.3 vs 69.9 and recall 0.454 vs 0.437, while overall MAE is 24.40 vs 24.35 and false alerts are unchanged (4.9%).
  * Onset recall fell, from 0.32 to 0.29 on test (0.30 vs 0.32 on validation). The selection rule did not include onset recall, and the variant trades some onset detection for better Severe-day accuracy.
* **Persistence blend:** the weight chosen on 2020 was 1.0, meaning no persistence at all, consistent with the error correlations.
* **No variant solves the problem.** Persistence's Severe-day MAE (63.8) is still lower than every variant's except weight 5 (61.5) and quantile 0.7 (56.8), and those two cost the most on ordinary days.

**Unseen city** (held out with a 100 km buffer):
* **XGBoost:** the penalty is 1.86 AQI points in Delhi (interval 0.62–3.14, 5% of its MAE), 0.48 in Mumbai, 0.40 in Kolkata and 0.24 in Bengaluru. All four intervals exclude 0.
  * For the other eight cities the interval includes 0, with point estimates from −0.16 to +0.24.
* **Random Forest** gives the same picture: Delhi +1.76; Bengaluru, Mumbai, Kolkata and Lucknow +0.31 to +0.43 with intervals excluding 0; the rest include 0.
* **Why Delhi is largest** (a plausible reading, not tested): its buffer removes 42 nearby stations, i.e. nearly all of the similar high-pollution training data.

**Unseen region:**
* The penalty is larger, and established for every region and both models.
* For XGBoost it ranges from +0.41 (Central) to +0.73 (West), and reaches +2.23 (1.53–2.96) for the North (IGP).
* Holding out the IGP also halves the training set (152k rows against roughly 245k–300k for the other hold-outs) and removes most of the high-pollution regime.

**Held-out models still beat persistence.**
* For XGBoost this holds in every city and every region: e.g. Delhi 37.5 vs 44.4, and the IGP 32.6 vs 36.6.
* The only exception is Random Forest in the North-East & Hills (20.74 vs 20.49).

**Conclusion, limited to the stated scenario.** If a new city or region has monitors reporting its own recent AQI and pollutants live, a model trained elsewhere forecasts it almost as well as one trained with its history.
* The penalty is at most about 1 AQI point everywhere except Delhi and the IGP, where it is about 2.
* This does not show that AQI can be predicted where there is no monitor.

## 8. User interface

`streamlit run app/app.py` is a **historical forecast demonstration** that replays the archive with models trained up to 2021. It has no live data ingestion. Dates before 2022 are labelled in-sample. A preflight check lists any missing pipeline files.

1. **Forecast:**
   * the production model's next-day AQI (chosen on 2021 validation), with an 80% range;
   * the observed AQI, if it is in the archive;
   * the **separate** Poor-or-worse alert from the production classifier: the score against its validation-tuned threshold, labelled as a model score, with an explanation whenever the alert and the AQI forecast disagree;
   * all models and the baselines side by side, and a 60-day history;
   * an XGBoost TreeSHAP waterfall whose bars, including "all other features", **sum to the model output**.
2. **Risk map:** the forecast category or the classifier alert for every station on a date, with the decision rule stated.
3. **What-if:** local (ICE-style) sensitivity of one station-day. Nothing changes until a slider moves, ranges always include the original value, and a reset button is provided.
4. **Benchmark:** tables, confidence intervals, PCA, backtest.
5. **Analyses:** A, C, E, severe days, spatial hold-outs.
6. **Data & method:** EDA, QA impact, coverage, the leakage checks and declarations, sources.

Missing values are shown as **"Unknown"**, never as a pollution category.

## 9. Limitations

* **Coordinates:** stations not matched to OpenAQ use city-centre coordinates. Fire distances and maps are coarse for them.
* **Data status:** CPCB data are provisional, and calibration drift is not corrected. The QA rules remove readings by rule, and their effect on extreme days is reported in section 3.2 rather than assumed harmless.
* **AQI definition:** the target is reconstructed with the CPCB breakpoints from project-cleaned data. The Severe-band upper concentrations and the 500 cap are conventions, so Severe days are censored.
* **Fires:** FIRMS counts are a lower bound, because clouds and haze hide fires. They are distance counts, not modelled smoke transport, and are based on the archived standard product.
* **Weather:** only observed weather up to D is used. A weather forecast for D+1 would be the natural extension, but must come from forecasts actually issued before the forecast time.
* **Evaluation:** scores are conditional on the station reporting the next day (section 2.2).
* **Lockdown flag:** the COVID-19 lockdown flag is always 0 in the validation and test periods. It is kept only so that the 2020 anomaly is not learned as ordinary seasonality; it carries no information for the test forecasts.

## 10. Reproducibility

* **Environment:** pinned in `requirements.txt` (tested environment).
* **Run manifests:** every benchmark run writes `reports/run_manifest.json`, recording the git commit, package versions, hardware, cutoffs, seed, chosen parameters, round counts and thresholds, and a hash of the feature table.
* **Runs, steps and timings:** listed in the [README](../README.md).
* **Configuration:** paths, AQI bands, QA thresholds and split dates are in `src/config.py`.
* **Model definitions:** shared by every script, in `src/zoo.py`.
* **Partial runs:** `--only` writes to `reports/partial/` and `models/partial/`, and never overwrites the full results.
